Section 05

Discussion

What the results support, what two published studies of the same gene add to them, and the honest account of where the analysis is weakest.

Interpretation

The analysis set out to test one gene, chosen in advance. Four things came back, and they are worth separating by how firmly they stand.

A note on figure numbers. The figures on this page are reproduced from the two papers discussed below and are numbered 1–4 in their own sequence. My own figures live on the Results page and are cited here as Results Figure 1 to Results Figure 5.

PPP4C is raised in breast tumour tissue, and that is the solid part. Every one of the five cohorts in Results Figure 1 puts the tumour box above the normal box, on four different measurement platforms, with fold-changes from +0.56 to +1.34 log₂ units and a rank-aggregated adjusted p of 3.8×10⁻³. Results Figure 2 then closes the loophole that different people, rather than different tissue, produce that gap: in 109 of 113 TCGA patients who contributed both a tumour and their own normal sample, PPP4C rises inside the individual. The four exceptions move by −0.09 to −0.21 log₂ units, which is noise rather than a subgroup going the other way.

It is a pan-breast change that subtype tilts, not a subtype-specific one. Across the five PAM50 groups in Results Figure 4 the medians span 6.48 to 7.27 — 0.79 log₂ units — against a tumour-versus-normal shift of +1.34 in the same cohort. PPP4C is elevated in every subtype, highest in HER2-enriched and lowest in Normal-like, and Luminal A against Basal-like is the clearest null in the whole set. That matters for how the result is described: this is not a finding about one kind of breast cancer.

It is not the loudest signal in the transcriptome, and it was never claimed to be. Results Figure 3 places PPP4C well inside the significant region but nowhere near the extremes of 39,393 tested genes. A gene does not have to be an outlier to be worth following; it does have to be reproducible, which is what Results Figures 1 and 2 establish.

The link to outcome is real but modest, and the adjusted version is borderline. The Kaplan–Meier curves in Results Figure 5 separate early and finish 5.1 percentage points apart at five years (84.5% against 79.4%), log-rank p = 0.021, hazard ratio 1.60 (95% CI 1.07–2.39). Adjusted for age, AJCC stage and PAM50 subtype the hazard ratio is 1.53 (95% CI 1.00–2.35, p = 0.0501). The confidence interval touches 1. The fair reading is that PPP4C carries prognostic information which is largely, though not entirely, shared with stage and subtype — a hypothesis worth testing in a second cohort, not a validated independent marker.

What none of that can do is say why. Every figure on the results page is an association measured in bulk tissue from patients whose tumours were already there. The question of whether PPP4C does anything — whether removing it changes how a breast cancer cell behaves — cannot be answered by this design at all. Two published studies can, and they are the subject of the next section.

What the published work adds

Two papers bear directly on this analysis. Xie et al. (2022) screened the whole phosphoprotein phosphatase catalytic (PPPCs) family in breast cancer and concluded that PPP1CA and PPP4C were the two members with real clinical significance [1]. That is an independent route to the same gene: their starting point was a family-wide screen, this project's was a single pre-registered target, and the two arrive at PPP4C from opposite directions. Li et al. (2026) then studied PPP4C on its own in thyroid carcinoma and triple-negative breast cancer, combining bioinformatics with tissue staining and cell-line experiments [2]. Between them they supply the two things this analysis is missing: measurement at the protein level, and evidence that runs in the causal direction.

Why this matters for Results Figure 1. My cohorts show PPP4C sitting higher in tumour tissue than in normal tissue. That is compatible with PPP4C driving something and equally compatible with it being swept along by whatever else a tumour cell is doing. Here the gene is removed and the cells slow down: growth curves separate by day 3 and end roughly half as high, and wound closure falls with every one of three independent siRNAs. Three siRNAs matter — a single construct can miss its target and hit something else, three agreeing is much harder to dismiss as off-target.

And for Results Figure 4. The two lines used are MCF-7, which is ER-positive and luminal, and MDA-MB-468, which is triple-negative and basal. Both respond. That sits comfortably beside my PAM50 result, where PPP4C was raised in every subtype rather than confined to one.

This answers the two easiest objections to Results Figures 1 and 2. The first is that everything I measured is messenger RNA, and mRNA is not protein — a transcript can rise without the protein following. Here the readout is the protein itself, stained in tissue, and the histoscore median moves from about 3 in adjacent tissue to about 8 in tumour. The second is study design, and the design here is the tissue-level twin of Results Figure 2: tumour compared against adjacent non-tumour tissue from the same specimens, so the comparison is within patients rather than between groups of them.

One difference to keep in view. Their cohort is triple-negative only. Results Figure 1 is all breast cancer. The agreement is about direction, and the direction is the part that replicates.

The breadth agrees with Results Figure 4; the ordering does not, and should not be forced to. Every cancer line sits above the non-tumorigenic MCF-10A — the luminal line MCF7, the HER2-amplified SK-BR-3 and both triple-negative lines — which is the cell-culture version of my finding that PPP4C is raised in all five PAM50 subtypes. But their two highest lines are the triple-negative ones, whereas in my 1,083 tumours the highest median was HER2-enriched at 7.27 with Basal-like mid-pack near 6.9.

I would not read that as a contradiction, because the two things are not comparable: five immortalised lines that have been grown in plastic for decades are not a sample of anything, least of all of subtype proportions in patients. The claim their panel supports is "raised across subtypes", which matches. The claim about which subtype is highest rests on my cohort, not on theirs.

This is the causal arm my design cannot supply. The first limitation listed below — association is not causation — is a real weakness of an analysis built on patient tissue that was collected once. Here the direction of the experiment is fixed by the experimenter: PPP4C is removed first, and proliferation, colony formation, migration and invasion all fall afterwards. In these two cell lines the arrow runs from PPP4C to behaviour, not the reverse.

A candidate account of Results Figure 5. If PPP4C supports growth and invasion, then tumours in my PPP4C-high half would be expected to behave more aggressively, which is what a hazard ratio of 1.60 describes. That is a plausible mechanism, not a demonstrated one: nothing in this figure was measured in my patients, and a cell line crossing a transwell membrane is a long way from a tumour metastasising.

Where the agreement stops

It would be easy to present the four figures above as confirmation and stop there. Three things argue against reading them that way.

The agreement is not fully independent

Xie et al. built their analysis on the same public databases this project used, TCGA among them. Where their expression conclusions and mine agree, part of that agreement is two analyses of overlapping data. Their cell-line experiments and Li et al.'s stained tissue are genuinely new evidence; the database sections are less so.

Nobody here tested prognosis in my cohort

Both papers report worse outcome with higher PPP4C, and my adjusted hazard ratio of 1.53 lands at p = 0.0501. Neither of their survival analyses is an independent test of my model, on my patients, with my covariates. The borderline result stays borderline.

Cell lines are not tumours

Figures 1, 3 and 4 above are five cell lines in culture. They have no immune system, no stroma and no treatment history, and they answer a question about what PPP4C can do — not about what it is doing in the 1,570 tumours I measured.

The cell-of-origin question is still open

Li et al.'s staining shows PPP4C protein in tumour tissue, which is more than bulk RNA can show, but my own data still cannot say which cells in the mixture carry the signal. That limitation survives both papers intact.

Limitations

These apply regardless of what the results turn out to be, and are worth stating plainly rather than burying.

Association, not causation

If PPP4C expression tracks with survival, that does not make it a driver of it. Expression differences can be a consequence of tumour biology, of treatment, or of what cell types happen to be in the sample. The knockdown work in Figures 1 and 4 above addresses this in cells; it does not import causation into my cohort.

One cohort, mostly

TCGA-BRCA is a convenience sample with its own recruitment and demographic biases. The four GEO cohorts help, but the survival analysis rests on TCGA alone, and both published studies lean on the same public resources.

Bulk tissue, mixed cells

Bulk RNA-seq averages tumour cells, immune cells and stroma together. A signal that looks like a tumour-cell change may really be a shift in immune infiltration.

Multiple testing and researcher choice

PPP4C was chosen before the analysis, which avoids the worst of this. The genes reported as correlated with it were not: they are the strongest of twenty thousand tested, so their coefficients are inflated.

Median splits lose information

Splitting patients at the median PPP4C value makes a readable Kaplan-Meier plot and a weaker test. The continuous Cox model is the primary analysis for that reason.

Incomplete clinical annotation

Treatment details are patchy in the public clinical tables, so survival differences cannot be fully separated from differences in what patients received.

Ninety-nine events

The whole survival section rests on 99 deaths inside the five-year window. That is what makes the confidence intervals as wide as they are, and no amount of supporting literature narrows them.

Transcript, not protein

Everything I measured is mRNA. Figure 2 above shows the protein moving the same way in someone else's cohort, which is reassuring, but it is not a protein-level check on my own samples.

What I would do next

  1. Re-run the survival models with treatment covariates wherever the annotation allows it, since 99 events and a p of 0.0501 is exactly the situation where an unmodelled confounder decides the answer.
  2. Check PPP4C protein in a public proteomic cohort such as CPTAC-BRCA, which would be the protein-level test on patient tumours that Figure 2 supplies only for triple-negative disease.
  3. Deconvolve the bulk expression into cell-type proportions to test whether the PPP4C signal is immune-driven, which neither published paper settles.
  4. Add a single-cell dataset to check which cell population actually expresses PPP4C.
  5. Repeat the tumour-versus-normal and survival analysis in the triple-negative subset alone, so that my cohort and Li et al.'s are asking the same question of the same disease.
  6. Test whether the genes co-expressed with PPP4C in my data overlap the WNT signalling, antigen presentation and DNA repair programmes that Xie et al. report for this family [1].
  7. Check whether PPP4C copy number at 16p11.2 explains the expression differences, rather than transcriptional regulation.
  8. Pre-register the thresholds and the analysis plan before touching any further validation cohort.
