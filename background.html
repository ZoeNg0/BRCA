Section 01

Background

The scientific background needed to follow this project, covering breast cancer and its molecular subtypes, what gene expression data measures, the PPP4C gene and the TCGA database.

Why breast cancer

How common breast cancer is, and why it suited a project built on public data.

Breast cancer was the most commonly diagnosed cancer worldwide in 2020. It's also one of the most studied cancers at the molecular level, which made it a good choice for this project: there is a lot of well-annotated public data and plenty of published research to compare against. Finding results that match earlier studies is also a useful check that an analysis is working properly.

According to GLOBOCAN 2020 estimates, there were about 2.3 million new cases that year, 11.7% of all cancer diagnoses, and breast cancer was the fifth leading cause of cancer death worldwide. Treatments such as targeted therapy, radiotherapy and immunotherapy have improved outcomes, but survival is much lower once the cancer has spread to distant organs, with roughly 20% of these patients surviving five years in commonly cited estimates. This is one reason researchers continue to look for markers that help identify which patients are at higher risk.

Patients with the same diagnosis can also have very different outcomes. Two tumours of the same size and stage may behave differently, and part of that difference comes from which genes are active in the tumour. Gene expression data can measure these differences, but a microscope can't.

Molecular subtypes of breast cancer

The five PAM50 subtypes and how they relate to prognosis and treatment.

When breast tumours are grouped by their gene expression patterns instead of by how they look, they fall into a small number of recurring groups called intrinsic subtypes. The standard system has five, usually assigned using a 50-gene test called PAM50.

Subtype affects treatment decisions, such as whether a patient is offered hormone therapy, HER2-targeted drugs, or chemotherapy alone. For this project, it means it's important to check whether a change in PPP4C is limited to one subtype or seen across all of them.

What gene expression data measures

What RNA sequencing measures and how the data has to be prepared before analysis.

Every cell in the body has the same DNA, but different cells use different genes. When a gene is active, it is copied (transcribed) into RNA. RNA sequencing counts these RNA molecules, giving roughly twenty thousand numbers per sample, one for each gene. Each number estimates how active that gene was when the sample was collected.

These numbers measure RNA, not protein. RNA levels often follow protein levels, but not always, and neither directly shows how active an enzyme is. All the measurements in this project are RNA levels.

Working with this kind of data involves three main steps. The counts are normalised, because samples that were sequenced more deeply produce higher counts overall. Genes with very low counts are removed, because their measurements are unreliable. And because thousands of genes are tested at once, p-values are adjusted for multiple testing; otherwise hundreds of genes would appear significant just by chance.

PPP4C and protein phosphatase 4

What PPP4C does, the subunits it works with, and the earlier studies that led to choosing it.

Many proteins in a cell are switched on or off by adding or removing phosphate groups (phosphorylation). Enzymes called phosphatases remove these phosphate groups. Protein phosphatase 4 (PP4) is one of them, and it is involved in DNA damage repair, in the checkpoints that stop a damaged cell from dividing, and in the cell cycle.

PPP4C is the catalytic subunit of PP4, meaning the part that carries out the chemical reaction. On its own it does very little. The regulatory subunits it binds to (PPP4R1, PPP4R2, PPP4R3A and others) determine which proteins it acts on, so PPP4C RNA levels alone can't show how active PP4 is in a tumour. This also suggests a possible check: if the PPP4C measurements reflect real biology and not noise, the regulatory subunits might be expected to rise and fall with it across tumours.

Previous research on PPP4C

I chose PPP4C because of earlier research. Xie et al. (2022) screened the whole family of phosphoprotein phosphatase catalytic subunits in breast cancer and found that PPP4C was one of only two members linked to clinical features and prognosis. Genes that changed alongside it were involved in WNT signalling, antigen presentation and DNA repair, and reducing PPP4C with siRNA slowed the growth and migration of breast cancer cell lines.

A more recent study by Li et al. (2026) looked at PPP4C in triple-negative breast cancer and thyroid cancer. It found more PPP4C protein in tumour tissue than in nearby normal tissue, worse overall survival in patients with higher PPP4C, and reduced growth, migration and invasion when PPP4C was knocked down in cultured cells. Both studies are compared with my results on the Discussion page.

Based on this research, I expected PPP4C to be higher in tumour tissue, and higher expression to be associated with worse survival. Because the prediction was made before the analysis, the results could have contradicted it. Most of the earlier evidence came from cell lines or single cohorts, so testing it across several independent datasets was the main way this project could add to it.

The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA)

The public database that provided the main dataset for this project.

The Cancer Genome Atlas is a public database with molecular and clinical data from more than eleven thousand tumours across thirty-three cancer types. Its breast cancer dataset, TCGA-BRCA, links each patient's gene expression data with de-identified clinical information such as stage, receptor status and follow-up time. Having expression and survival data for the same patients is what makes survival analysis possible.

All data used here is open-access and de-identified. No controlled-access or individually identifiable data is involved.
