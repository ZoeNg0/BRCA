Section 01

Background

The basics behind this project: breast cancer, its molecular subtypes, gene expression data, the PPP4C gene and the TCGA database.

Why breast cancer

Breast cancer is the most commonly diagnosed cancer in the world, and it's also one of the most studied at the molecular level. For a student project that's useful. There's a lot of well-labelled public data, plus decades of published results to compare against. If my analysis finds something that's already known, that's a good sign the pipeline is working.

In 2020 there were an estimated 2.3 million new cases, 11.7% of all cancer diagnoses, and it was the fifth leading cause of cancer death worldwide. Targeted therapy, radiotherapy and immunotherapy have improved outcomes a lot, but only about 20% of patients whose cancer has spread survive five years. So there's still a need for markers that predict which patients will do badly.

Outcomes also vary a lot within the same diagnosis. Two tumours of the same size and stage can behave very differently, and much of that comes down to which genes the tumour is expressing, which you can't see under a microscope. Expression data can pick up those differences, and the next section covers how.

The intrinsic molecular subtypes

When tumours are grouped by which genes they express rather than by how they look, a few groups keep showing up. The standard system has five, usually assigned using a 50-gene classifier called PAM50.

The subtype affects treatment. It helps decide whether a patient gets hormone therapy, HER2-targeted antibodies, or chemotherapy alone, so a gene that moves tumours between groups would be useful to know about.

What gene expression data is

Every cell has the same DNA, but different cells copy (transcribe) different parts of it into RNA. RNA sequencing counts those RNA molecules, so each tumour ends up as about twenty thousand numbers, one per gene. Each number estimates how active that gene was when the sample was frozen.

This has three consequences for the analysis. Counts depend on how deeply each sample was sequenced, so they need to be normalised before samples can be compared. Genes with low counts are noisy, so they get filtered out. And when you test twenty thousand genes at once you get hundreds of false positives by chance, so the p-values have to be corrected for multiple testing.

The target gene: PPP4C

Protein phosphatase 4 (PP4) is an enzyme complex that removes phosphate groups from other proteins. Cells use phosphorylation to switch many of their proteins on and off, and phosphatases reverse it. PP4 is involved in DNA damage repair, the checkpoints that decide whether a damaged cell can divide, and the cell cycle.

PPP4C is the catalytic subunit, meaning the part of the complex that carries out the reaction. By itself it doesn't do much. The regulatory subunits it binds to (PPP4R1, PPP4R2, PPP4R3A and others) decide which proteins it acts on. This gave me a check I could run: if the PPP4C measurements are real signal and not noise, the regulatory subunits should go up and down with it across tumours.

Previous studies

I didn't choose PPP4C at random. Its gene family, the phosphoprotein phosphatase catalytic subunits, has been studied in breast cancer before. When the whole family was screened for clinical relevance, PPP4C was one of only two members that stood out. It was linked to clinical and pathological features and to prognosis, and the genes changing alongside it were involved in WNT signalling, antigen presentation and DNA repair. Silencing it with siRNA slowed growth and migration in breast cancer cell lines.

A more recent study looked at PPP4C by itself in triple-negative breast cancer and thyroid cancer. It found more PPP4C protein in tumours than in nearby normal tissue, worse overall survival in patients with higher expression, and reduced growth, migration and invasion when the gene was knocked down in cell culture. I compare both studies with my results, including their figures, on the Discussion page.

This gives my project a clear prediction. If the published findings are right, PPP4C should be higher in tumour tissue here too, and higher expression should go with worse outcomes. Because the prediction has a direction, it can be proven wrong. Most of the earlier work also used cell lines or single cohorts, so testing it across several cohorts and platforms adds something new, and that's what the rest of the site does.

What TCGA is

The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) is a public archive with molecular and clinical data from over eleven thousand tumours across thirty-three cancer types. Its breast cancer cohort, TCGA-BRCA, links expression data to de-identified clinical records such as stage, receptor status and follow-up time. Having both in one place is what makes survival analysis possible on a laptop.

All data used here is open-access and de-identified. No controlled-access or individually identifiable data is involved.
