Section 01

Background

Why breast cancer

Breast cancer is the most commonly diagnosed cancer worldwide. It is also one of the best-characterised at the molecular level, which makes it a good subject for a student project: the public data is large, well annotated, and there are decades of published findings to check your own results against. If an analysis recovers something already known, that is evidence the pipeline works, not a wasted effort.

The scale behind that first sentence: an estimated 2.3 million new cases in 2020, 11.7% of all cancer diagnoses, and the fifth leading cause of cancer death worldwide. Targeted therapy, radiotherapy and immunotherapy have improved outcomes substantially, but roughly 20% of patients whose disease has spread survive only five years, which is why the search for markers that predict who will do badly has not stopped.

Survival also differs sharply within the diagnosis. Two patients with tumours of the same size and stage can have very different outcomes, and much of that difference tracks with which genes their tumours express rather than with anything visible under a microscope. That is what makes expression data worth reading, and it is the subject of the next section.

The intrinsic molecular subtypes

Sorting tumours by which genes they switch on, rather than by how they look under a microscope, produces a small number of recurring groups. The standard scheme recognises five, usually assigned with a 50-gene classifier called PAM50.

These labels matter clinically. They decide whether a patient is offered hormone therapy, HER2-targeted antibodies, or chemotherapy alone, which is why a gene that shifts a tumour between groups is worth finding.

What gene expression data is

Every cell carries the same DNA but transcribes different parts of it into RNA. RNA sequencing counts those transcripts, so a tumour becomes a vector of roughly twenty thousand numbers: one per gene, each an estimate of how actively that gene was being read at the moment the sample was frozen.

Three consequences shape everything downstream. Counts depend on how deeply a sample was sequenced, so they must be normalised before samples can be compared. Counts are noisy for lowly-expressed genes, so weak genes are filtered out. And testing twenty thousand genes at once produces false positives by the hundred, so p-values must be corrected for multiple testing.

The target gene: PPP4C

Protein phosphatase 4 is an enzyme complex that removes phosphate groups from other proteins. Phosphorylation is how a cell switches many of its proteins on and off, so a phosphatase is a switch in the other direction, and PP4 sits on switches that matter: DNA damage repair, the checkpoints that decide whether a damaged cell divides, and progression through the cell cycle.

PPP4C is the catalytic subunit — the part of the complex that does the chemistry. On its own it does very little; which proteins it acts on is decided by the regulatory subunits it binds (PPP4R1, PPP4R2, PPP4R3A and others). That has a practical consequence for this project: if the PPP4C measurements are real signal rather than noise, the regulatory subunits should rise and fall alongside it across tumours, which is a check the analysis can actually run.

What is already published

PPP4C was not picked at random. The phosphoprotein phosphatase catalytic family has been examined across breast cancer before, and when the whole family was screened for clinical significance, PPP4C came out as one of only two members that mattered — associated with clinicopathological features and prognosis, and with the differentially expressed genes around it falling into WNT signalling, antigen presentation and DNA repair. Silencing it with siRNA slowed proliferation and migration in breast cancer cell lines.

A more recent study looked at PPP4C on its own in triple-negative breast cancer and in thyroid carcinoma. It reported the protein raised in tumour tissue against adjacent normal tissue, higher expression associated with worse overall survival, and knockdown suppressing proliferation, migration and invasion in culture. Both studies are examined against this project's own results, with their figures reproduced, on the Discussion page.

Two things follow for this project. The prediction is directional and therefore falsifiable: if the published account holds, PPP4C should be raised in tumour tissue here too, and higher expression should associate with worse outcome. And the existing work is concentrated in cell lines and in single cohorts — which leaves room for an independent test across several cohorts and platforms, which is what the rest of this site is.

What TCGA is

The Cancer Genome Atlas is a public archive of molecular and clinical data from more than eleven thousand tumours across thirty-three cancer types. The breast cancer cohort, TCGA-BRCA, pairs expression data with de-identified clinical records including stage, receptor status and follow-up time. That pairing is what makes survival analysis possible from a laptop.

All data used here is open-access and de-identified. No controlled-access or individually identifiable data is involved.
