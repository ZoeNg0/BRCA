PPP4C expression in tumour and normal tissue

Figure 1 compares tumour and normal tissue in each of the five datasets.

The first question was whether PPP4C RNA levels are higher in breast tumours than in normal breast tissue. This was tested in five independent datasets measured on four different platforms. A difference seen in only one dataset could be caused by that dataset's particular samples or technology, so finding the same direction of change in all five makes the result much more reliable.

Each dataset is plotted on its own axis, in its own units. The datasets were measured with different technologies (RNA-seq counts, FPKM, and two types of microarray), so their raw values aren't on the same scale. Converting them to a common scale would mean transforming the data, which could create differences that aren't real. Each panel therefore compares a dataset's tumour samples only with its own normal samples.

Why the size of the change varies between datasets. The microarray datasets GSE70947 and GSE86374 show the smallest increases (+0.56 and +0.84 log₂ units), compared with +1.34 in TCGA. This is expected from the technology: microarrays measure a narrower range of expression levels than RNA-seq, so large changes appear smaller. This project's validation report notes the same effect. The direction of the change is consistent across platforms, but its size shouldn't be compared between them.

The main evidence that PPP4C RNA is raised in tumours is that all five independent analyses agree. Combining them by rank aggregation gives an adjusted p-value of 3.8×10⁻³, with the change in the same direction in all five datasets.

PPP4C in matched tumour and normal samples

Figure 2 compares tumour and normal samples taken from the same 113 TCGA patients.

Comparing tumours from one group of people with normal tissue from another has a weakness: differences between the people themselves could affect the result. In TCGA, all 113 normal samples come from patients who also have a tumour sample, so each patient can serve as their own control. Figure 2 tests whether PPP4C is higher in a person's tumour than in their own normal tissue.

Patients with lower PPP4C in their tumour. PPP4C was higher in the tumour in 109 of 113 patients. In the other four, the decrease was small (between −0.09 and −0.21 log₂ units) and within the range of normal measurement variation, so these patients show no meaningful change rather than a real decrease. They are drawn at full strength in the figure so they aren't hidden by the overall trend.

Paired and unpaired tests. This figure uses a paired test, but the TCGA differential expression analysis behind Figures 1 and 3 was run unpaired on the same samples. A paired analysis would probably give an even smaller p-value, but the unpaired result is reported as it was actually run. The tumour median also differs slightly between the two figures (6.91 vs 6.79) because Figure 1 includes all 1,111 tumours, while this figure includes only the 113 with a matched normal sample.

PPP4C compared with all other genes

Figure 3 places PPP4C among all the genes tested in the TCGA tumour-versus-normal comparison.

PPP4C was one of thousands of genes compared between tumour and normal tissue in TCGA. Figure 3 shows all of them, with PPP4C circled. It sits well within the significant region but far from the most extreme genes, so its change is consistent and statistically strong, but not unusually large compared with the rest of the genome.

PPP4C across breast cancer subtypes

Figure 4 checks whether the increase in PPP4C is limited to a particular subtype.

An overall difference between tumour and normal tissue could be driven by a large change in just one subtype. For example, if PPP4C were raised only in basal-like tumours, the earlier results would describe that subtype and not breast cancer in general. Figure 4 compares PPP4C across the five PAM50 subtypes to check this.

Differences between subtypes. PPP4C was raised in every subtype. Subtype medians ranged from 6.48 (Normal-like) to 7.27 (HER2-enriched), a spread of 0.79 log₂ units, which is smaller than the +1.34 difference between tumour and normal tissue in the same cohort. Luminal A and Basal-like did not differ significantly (adjusted p = 0.39). This suggests that the increase in PPP4C is shared across breast cancer, with subtype having a smaller effect on its level.

Why this figure uses 1,083 patients. PAM50 is assigned once per patient, so the sixteen duplicate aliquots kept in Figure 1 were merged first (1,111 → 1,095). Another twelve patients have no PAM50 call and were left out, which leaves 1,083. The survival analysis below uses a smaller number again.

PPP4C expression and survival

Figure 5 tests whether PPP4C expression is associated with five-year overall survival.

Patients were split at the median PPP4C value into high and low groups and followed for five years. After five years, too few patients remain in follow-up for the curves to be reliable.

Survival results. The curves separate early and stay apart. At five years, survival was 79.4% in the PPP4C-high group and 84.5% in the PPP4C-low group, a difference of 5.1 percentage points (log-rank p = 0.021). The unadjusted hazard ratio was 1.60 (95% CI 1.07–2.39), meaning patients in the high group had about a 60% higher rate of death during follow-up. After adjusting for age, AJCC stage and PAM50 subtype, the hazard ratio was 1.53 (95% CI 1.00–2.35, p = 0.0501, n = 1,022). The confidence interval reaches 1, which would mean no difference, and the p-value is just above the usual 0.05 threshold.

What this shows. Higher PPP4C RNA is associated with worse five-year survival in this cohort. After adjustment, the estimated effect stays similar in size but is no longer clearly statistically significant, so these data can't show whether PPP4C predicts survival independently of stage, age and subtype. This is an association, not evidence that PPP4C causes worse survival. Even if it were confirmed, a 5.1-point difference between groups would not make PPP4C a useful clinical test on its own. The result needs to be tested in another cohort.

Statistical limitations. The survival analysis is based on 99 deaths, which is why the confidence intervals are wide. The proportional hazards assumption holds for the unadjusted model shown in the plot (Schoenfeld global p = 0.061) but is weaker for the adjusted model, so the adjusted hazard ratio is best read as an average effect over the five years.

Biological processes in the tumour gene network

Figure 6 tests which biological processes the most connected genes in the tumour network are involved in.

The previous figures focus on PPP4C itself. Figure 6 looks at the wider set of genes that differ between tumour and normal tissue, to see which biological processes they are organised around. Each gene that passed the Figure 3 thresholds was treated as a node, and two genes were connected if their expression was strongly correlated across the 1,095 tumours (|Spearman ρ| ≥ 0.70). The 200 most connected genes in the resulting 4,563-gene network are the hubs. These hubs were tested against 32 curated gene sets in two groups: proliferation, and EMT / motility.

PPP4C is not one of the hubs. It is part of the network, but with six connections it ranks 811th, while the hubs have between 71 and 172. This figure therefore describes the wider tumour network that PPP4C belongs to, not PPP4C's own connections.

Proliferation gene sets. Eight of the nine proliferation sets were significant, most by a wide margin: G2M checkpoint (23.35×), E2F targets (22.33×) and mitotic nuclear division (17.12×). Reactome's cell cycle set alone contains 71 of the 200 hubs. The most connected genes in the tumour network are mainly involved in cell division.

EMT and motility gene sets. Four of the twenty-three EMT and motility sets were significant, all with small overlaps: angiogenesis (8.5×, three genes), the integrin-1 pathway (5.58×, four genes), the EMT hallmark (4.08×, nine genes) and GO cell migration (1.65×). An enrichment of 1.65× is only slightly above chance, even though it passed the significance threshold. GO wound healing was depleted (0.45×), and four sets (TGF-β signalling, apical junction, integrin signalling and smooth muscle contraction) contained no hub genes. Overall, motility-related genes are largely missing from the hubs. Showing only significant results (FDR < 0.05) would hide this pattern, which is why non-significant sets are kept in the figure.

Limitations of this figure. The 32 gene sets overlap considerably. For example, Reactome's cell cycle set contains its own mitotic subset, and GO cell migration and cell motility share all 26 of their hub genes. These are therefore not 32 independent tests, and the multiple-testing correction is only approximate. Enrichments based on three or four genes (every significant EMT set except cell migration) could change a lot if a single gene were added or removed. The hub list also depends on the correlation threshold, and a lower threshold than |ρ| ≥ 0.70 would produce a different network.

Comparison with previous research. Knockdown experiments described on the Discussion page found that reducing PPP4C slows migration and invasion as well as growth in cultured cells. These results don't contradict that. Figure 6 describes which processes dominate the co-expression network in bulk tumour tissue, which is a different question from what PPP4C does inside individual cells. It does mean this dataset gives little independent support for a role in cell movement.
