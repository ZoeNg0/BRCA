PPP4C in tumour vs normal tissue

The first question was whether PPP4C is higher in tumour tissue than in normal breast tissue. Protein phosphatase 4 has been reported as raised in several solid tumours, and this comparison was the basis for choosing the gene, so I started here. One cohort on its own would only be an observation, so I checked it in five independent cohorts measured on four different platforms.

Each cohort is plotted on its own axis and in its own units. The five datasets come from four platforms (RNA-seq counts, FPKM, and two generations of microarray), and their raw PPP4C values aren't on the same scale. Putting them on one axis would mean transforming the data first, and then differences could come from the transformation instead of the biology. With separate panels, each one just shows that cohort's tumours against its own normal samples.

Effect sizes by platform. The array cohorts GSE70947 and GSE86374 have the smallest shifts (+0.56 and +0.84 log₂ units), compared with +1.34 in the TCGA RNA-seq cohort. This comes from the measurement rather than the biology, since microarrays have a narrower dynamic range and compress large fold changes. This project's validation report flags the same thing. The direction is consistent across platforms, but the size of the shift shouldn't be compared between them.

The main evidence that PPP4C is raised is that five independent differential expression results agree. Combined by rank aggregation, the adjusted p is 3.8×10⁻³, with the same direction in all five. The conclusion doesn't depend on any single panel's p-value.

Matched patient pairs

Comparing a group of tumours with a separate group of normal samples has a weakness: the difference could be due to who the patients are, and not to the tumour itself. TCGA gets around this because all 113 of its normal samples come from patients who also have a tumour sample. Each patient can then be their own control, and the question becomes whether PPP4C goes up within each person.

Patients where PPP4C went down. Four of the 113 patients go down. The changes are small, between −0.09 and −0.21 log₂ units, which is within normal measurement noise, so these look like patients with no real change rather than a subgroup going the opposite way. I've kept them visible because a plot showing only the median would hide them.

Paired vs unpaired testing. This figure uses a paired test, but the TCGA differential expression in Figures 1 and 3 was run unpaired on the same samples. A paired analysis is the stronger design and would probably give an even smaller p-value, but I've reported the unpaired result as it was actually run. The tumour median also differs between the two figures (6.91 vs 6.79) because Figure 1 uses all 1,111 tumours and this one only uses the 113 with a matched normal.

Genome-wide comparison

PPP4C was picked from a genome-wide comparison of tumour and normal tissue, and this plot shows all the genes it was picked from. Each point is a gene, with PPP4C circled. It's well within the significant region but not near the extremes, so it's a reproducible signal without being one of the strongest on the plot.

PPP4C by subtype

An overall tumour-vs-normal difference could be driven by a big change in just one subtype. If PPP4C were only raised in basal-like tumours, for example, the results above would really be about that subtype and not breast cancer in general. Comparing PPP4C across the five PAM50 subtypes checks for this.

Subtype differences. Subtype medians range from 6.48 to 7.27, a spread of 0.79 log₂ units, while the tumour-vs-normal shift in the same cohort is +1.34. PPP4C is raised in every subtype, highest in HER2-enriched and lowest in Normal-like, and the differences between subtypes are smaller than the tumour-vs-normal difference. Luminal A vs Basal-like shows no difference (adjusted p = 0.39). So the increase applies to breast cancer in general, with subtype shifting it slightly up or down.

Sample size. PAM50 is assigned per patient, so the sixteen duplicate aliquots kept in Figure 1 had to be merged first (1,111 → 1,095). Another twelve patients have no PAM50 call and were left out instead of being shown as a sixth group, which leaves 1,083. The survival analysis below uses a smaller number again.

Survival

Differences in expression only matter clinically if they're linked to outcome. I split the cohort at the median PPP4C value and followed both groups for five years. I stopped at five years because after that, not enough patients are still being followed for the curves to be reliable.

Survival results. The curves separate early and stay apart, ending 5.1 percentage points apart at five years (84.5% vs 79.4%), with a log-rank p of 0.021 and a hazard ratio of 1.60 (95% CI 1.07–2.39). After adjusting for age, AJCC stage and PAM50 subtype, the hazard ratio is 1.53 (95% CI 1.00–2.35, p = 0.0501, n = 1,022). The confidence interval touches 1 and the p-value is just above the usual 0.05 cutoff.

Interpretation. Patients with high PPP4C have worse survival, and adjusting for the main confounders doesn't remove the association, but it doesn't show it's independent either. Most, though not all, of the prognostic information in PPP4C overlaps with stage and subtype. It should be tested in a second cohort before being treated as an independent marker.

Caveats. The analysis is based on only 99 deaths, which is why the confidence intervals are wide. The proportional hazards assumption holds for the unadjusted (univariate) model shown in the plot (Schoenfeld global p = 0.061) but is weaker for the adjusted model, so the adjusted hazard ratio is best treated as an average over time.

Hub gene enrichment

The figures above are all about PPP4C. This one looks at the whole tumour-vs-normal signal to see what it's organised around, since a gene's role depends on the genes around it. Every gene that passed the Figure 3 cutoffs is a node, and two nodes are connected if their expression correlates at |Spearman ρ| ≥ 0.70 across the 1,095 tumours. The 200 most connected genes in the resulting 4,563-gene network are the hubs. I tested what those 200 genes are for using 32 curated gene sets in two groups: proliferation, and EMT / motility.

PPP4C isn't one of the hubs. It's in the network and passes the differential expression filter easily, but it only has six connections and ranks 811th, while the hubs have between 71 and 172. So this figure doesn't show PPP4C's own neighbourhood. It shows the wider network PPP4C is part of, which turns out to be very one-sided.

Proliferation sets. Eight of the nine proliferation sets pass correction, most by a wide margin: G2M checkpoint at 23.35×, E2F targets at 22.33×, and mitotic nuclear division at 17.12×. Reactome's cell cycle set alone contains 71 of the 200 hubs. The most connected genes in this network are mainly the ones involved in cell division.

EMT and motility sets. Of the twenty-three EMT and motility sets, four pass, and all of those are small: angiogenesis at 8.5× on three genes, the integrin-1 pathway at 5.58× on four, the EMT hallmark at 4.08× on nine, and GO cell migration at 1.65×, which is barely above chance even though the p-value passes. GO wound healing is depleted, at 0.45×, and four sets (TGF-β signalling, apical junction, integrin signalling and smooth muscle contraction) contain no hub genes. Motility mostly isn't represented among the hubs, and that's the main result of this figure. Filtering to FDR < 0.05 would hide it completely, which is why the failed sets stay on the plot.

Limitations of this figure. The 32 sets overlap a lot. Reactome's cell cycle set includes its own mitotic subset, and GO cell migration and cell motility share all 26 of their hub genes, so these aren't 32 independent tests and the correction is only approximate. Folds based on three or four genes (every EMT set that passed except cell migration) can change a lot if a single gene changes. The hub list also depends on the cutoff, and a looser threshold than |ρ| ≥ 0.70 would give a different network.

Compared with the published work. The knockdown experiments on the Discussion page found that removing PPP4C slows migration and invasion as well as growth, and nothing here contradicts that. This figure is about which programmes the hub structure of the bulk tumour network is built on, which is a question about co-expression in tissue. It doesn't test what a single gene does to a cell in culture. It does mean my data alone can't support much of a motility story.
