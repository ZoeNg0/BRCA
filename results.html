Is PPP4C expression shifted in tumours?

Before anything about outcome can be asked, the gene has to be doing something. Protein phosphatase 4 has been reported as elevated in several solid tumours, so the first test is the plainest one available: compare PPP4C in tumour tissue against normal breast tissue. One cohort answering that question is an observation; five independent cohorts, on four different measurement platforms, answering it the same way is a result. That is the comparison the target selection actually rested on, so it is where this section starts.

Each cohort is drawn on its own axis, in its own units. That is deliberate. These five datasets were measured on four different platforms — RNA-seq counts, FPKM, and two generations of microarray — and their raw PPP4C values do not share a scale. Putting them on one axis would mean transforming them first, and the transformation, rather than the biology, would be doing the work. Five separate panels need no such justification: each one is simply that cohort's tumours against that cohort's normals.

Why the effect sizes differ between panels. The array cohorts GSE70947 and GSE86374 show the smallest shifts — +0.56 and +0.84 log₂ units, against +1.34 in the RNA-seq TCGA cohort. That ordering is a property of the measurement rather than the biology: microarray dynamic range compresses large fold-changes, which this project's validation report flags independently. The direction is consistent on every platform; the magnitude is not, and should not be read as though it were.

The evidence that PPP4C is genuinely elevated is the agreement of five independent differential-expression results, combined by rank aggregation (RRA adjusted p = 3.8×10⁻³, same direction in all five) — not any one panel's p-value.

Does it hold inside individual patients?

Comparing one set of tumours against a different set of normal tissue leaves a loophole open: the difference could reflect who the patients are rather than what the tumour did. TCGA closes it. Every one of its 113 normal samples comes from a patient who also contributed a tumour, so each patient can serve as their own control, and the question sharpens — does PPP4C rise within a person, not merely on average across groups?

109 of 113 patients go up; four go down. The four exceptions are small — between −0.09 and −0.21 log₂ units, inside the range of ordinary measurement noise — so they read less as a subgroup moving the other way than as a handful of patients showing no real change. They are worth leaving visible: a figure showing only the median would not admit they exist.

One inconsistency worth stating. This figure uses a paired test, but the TCGA differential expression behind Figures 1 and 3 was run unpaired on the same samples. Pairing is the stronger design and would likely give a smaller p-value again; the unpaired result is reported as it was actually run rather than quietly upgraded here. The tumour median also differs between the two figures — 6.91 against 6.79 — because Figure 1 uses all 1,111 tumours and this one only the 113 that have a matched normal.

What else separates tumour from normal?

PPP4C did not come from a guess. It came out of a genome-wide comparison of tumour against normal tissue, and the field it was selected from is worth seeing. Each point below is one gene, and PPP4C is ringed: comfortably inside the significant region, but nowhere near the extremes. That is the honest picture of it — a reproducible signal, not the loudest one on the plot.

Is it one subtype doing the work?

A tumour-versus-normal difference can hide a large effect confined to one group. Breast cancer is not one disease, and if PPP4C were elevated only in, say, basal-like tumours, everything above would need rewriting as a statement about that subtype rather than about breast cancer. Reading PPP4C across the five PAM50 intrinsic subtypes separates those possibilities.

Subtype modulates the effect; it does not explain it. The subtype medians span 6.48 to 7.27 — a range of 0.79 log₂ units — while the tumour-versus-normal shift in the same cohort is +1.34. PPP4C is raised in every subtype, highest in HER2-enriched and lowest in Normal-like, and the difference between subtypes is the smaller effect. Luminal A against Basal-like is the clearest null in the whole set (adjusted p = 0.39). So this is a pan-breast change that subtype tilts, not a subtype-restricted one.

Why the n is not 1,111. PAM50 is called once per patient, so the sixteen duplicate aliquots that Figure 1 keeps have to collapse first (1,111 → 1,095), and twelve further patients carry no PAM50 call at all. They are dropped rather than shown as a sixth group, which is why this figure plots 1,083. The survival analysis below is smaller again.

Does PPP4C track with survival?

Expression differences only matter clinically if they connect to outcome. Splitting the cohort at the median PPP4C value and following both halves for five years asks the question directly. Five years is the window the analysis was restricted to: beyond it the numbers still at risk thin out and the curves stop being worth reading.

The separation is real but modest, and the adjusted result is borderline. The curves separate early and stay apart, ending 5.1 percentage points apart at five years (84.5% against 79.4%), with a log-rank p of 0.021 and a hazard ratio of 1.60 (95% CI 1.07–2.39). Adjusted for age, AJCC stage and PAM50 subtype, the hazard ratio is 1.53 (95% CI 1.00–2.35, p = 0.0501, n = 1,022) — the confidence interval touches 1 and the p-value lands just the wrong side of the conventional threshold.

What that means. PPP4C-high tumours do worse, and adjusting for the obvious confounders does not remove the association — but nor does it establish it as independent. The honest reading is that PPP4C carries prognostic information which is largely, though not entirely, shared with stage and subtype. It is a hypothesis worth testing in a second cohort, not a validated independent marker.

Two caveats on the statistics. The whole analysis rests on 99 events, which is what makes the confidence intervals as wide as they are. The proportional hazards assumption holds for the univariate model the curve depicts (Schoenfeld global p = 0.061) but is weaker for the adjusted model, so the adjusted hazard ratio should be read as a summary rather than a constant effect over time.

What is this tumour network built around?

Everything above is about one gene. This last figure steps back and asks what the whole tumour-versus-normal signal is organised around, because a gene means something different depending on the company it keeps. Every gene that cleared the Figure 3 thresholds becomes a node; any two nodes whose expression correlates at |Spearman ρ| ≥ 0.70 across the 1,095 tumours are joined by an edge; and the 200 most connected genes in the resulting 4,563-gene network are its hubs. The question this figure asks is what those 200 genes are for, tested against 32 curated gene sets split between two themes — proliferation, and EMT / motility.

One thing to be clear about before reading it: PPP4C is not one of the hubs. It is in the network, comfortably past the differential-expression filter, but with six connections it ranks 811th, against the 71 to 172 connections that the hub genes carry. So this is not a picture of PPP4C's own neighbourhood. It is the landscape PPP4C sits in, and it is worth showing because that landscape turns out to be heavily one-sided.

This network is a cell-cycle machine. Eight of the nine proliferation sets survive correction, and not narrowly: G2M checkpoint at 23.35×, E2F targets at 22.33×, mitotic nuclear division at 17.12×. Reactome's cell cycle set contains 71 of the 200 hubs on its own. Whatever else separates these tumours from normal tissue, the genes doing the connecting are the ones that run mitosis.

Motility mostly is not there, and that is the actual finding. Twenty-three EMT and motility sets were tested and four pass, all of them small: angiogenesis at 8.5× on three genes, the integrin-1 pathway at 5.58× on four, the EMT hallmark at 4.08× on nine, and GO cell migration at 1.65× — a fold that low is barely above chance even when the p-value clears. GO wound healing runs the other way entirely, at 0.45×, and four sets — TGF-β signalling, apical junction, integrin signalling and smooth muscle contraction — contain no hub gene at all. This is why the figure draws its failures: filter to FDR < 0.05 and the result disappears, because the result is the absence.

Three things it does not show. The 32 sets overlap heavily — Reactome's cell cycle contains its own mitotic subset, and GO cell migration and cell motility share all 26 of their hub genes — so these are not 32 independent tests and the correction across them is approximate. A fold built on three or four genes, which is every EMT set that passed except cell migration, moves a long way if one gene changes. And hub membership is a property of the threshold: at |ρ| ≥ 0.70 the list is this one, and a looser cutoff would draw a different network.

Read against the published work. The knockdown experiments discussed on the Discussion page report that removing PPP4C slows migration and invasion as well as growth. Nothing here contradicts that: this figure says motility programmes are not what the hub structure of the bulk tumour network is built on, which is a statement about co-expression in tissue, not about what a single gene can do to a cell in culture. It does temper how far the proliferation-and-motility story should be pushed from my data alone.
