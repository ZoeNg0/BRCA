PPP4C expression in breast cancer

This project uses public RNA sequencing data from 1,111 primary breast tumours in The Cancer Genome Atlas, plus four independent cohorts, to test whether PPP4C (the catalytic subunit of protein phosphatase 4) is raised in tumours, differs between subtypes, and tracks with how long patients survive. This page summarises the research question, aims and key findings, and links to each section of the website.

The question

Research question

The question this project set out to answer, why PPP4C was chosen, and the four aims of the analysis.

Is PPP4C expression raised in breast tumours, does it differ between the intrinsic molecular subtypes, and is it associated with patient survival independently of stage, age and subtype?

Breast cancer isn't a single disease. Tumours that look alike under a microscope can grow and respond to treatment very differently, and much of that variation is linked to gene expression, which is used to sort tumours into intrinsic subtypes. Public databases now hold gene expression and clinical data for thousands of patients, which made it possible for me to carry out this project using a laptop and freely available data.

I focused on PPP4C, the catalytic subunit of an enzyme called protein phosphatase 4 (PP4). PP4 helps control DNA damage repair and cell division, two processes that often go wrong in cancer, and earlier studies had reported higher PPP4C levels in several types of tumour. I wanted to find out whether that pattern held up in a large, independent set of breast cancer data.

My main dataset was TCGA-BRCA from The Cancer Genome Atlas, which has RNA sequencing and clinical data for over 1,000 breast cancer patients. I used four smaller datasets from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) to check whether the TCGA results could be repeated.

The project had four aims:

  1. Characterise the cohort by matching each sample to its patient and working out the correct sample size for each analysis.
  2. Compare PPP4C expression between tumours and normal breast tissue, and across the five PAM50 subtypes.
  3. Test whether PPP4C expression is associated with survival, and whether that association remains after accounting for stage, age and subtype.
  4. Place it in context by building a network of genes that change together in tumours and identifying the biological processes that network centres on.

Key findings

PPP4C RNA levels were higher in tumours than in normal tissue in all five datasets and in 109 of 113 patients with matched samples, and the increase was seen in every subtype. Patients with higher PPP4C had somewhat worse five-year survival, but after adjusting for stage, age and subtype the result was borderline (p = 0.0501), so it isn't clear whether PPP4C adds prognostic information on its own.

Process

Research process

The main stages of the project, in the order they were carried out.

  1. Choosing the gene

    I chose PPP4C before starting the analysis, instead of testing every gene and picking whichever looked best. I then read the published research on PP4 in cancer so I knew what results to expect.

  2. Preparing the TCGA data

    I downloaded gene expression, clinical and mutation data from the Genomic Data Commons (GDC) and matched 1,224 samples to 1,095 patients. The 113 patients who also had a normal tissue sample were kept as a matched comparison group.

  3. Comparing tumour and normal tissue

    I compared gene expression between tumour and normal samples using DESeq2, with significance thresholds set before looking at the results.

  4. Replicating the results

    I repeated the comparison in four GEO datasets from three other measurement platforms and combined the five results using rank aggregation.

  5. Subtype and survival analysis

    I compared PPP4C across the five PAM50 subtypes and tested its association with survival using Kaplan–Meier curves and a Cox model adjusted for stage, age and subtype.

  6. Gene network analysis

    I built a network of genes whose expression rises and falls together across tumours, and tested which biological processes its most connected genes belong to.

  7. Write-up

    I compared my findings with published studies, wrote up the limitations, and built this website.
